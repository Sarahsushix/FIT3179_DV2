Breast Cancer Screening in Australia

Exploring screening participation, regional inequalities, and women's health outcomes across Australia.

The National Picture

Breast cancer screening is an important part of women's preventive healthcare in Australia. Yet participation is not equal across the country. Explore how screening rates vary between states and local regions, and what these differences mean for access to preventive healthcare.

Mapping Non-Participation in Breast Screening Across Australia

Screening participation rates reveal where uptake is lower, but they do not show how many women are missing from screening programmes. This dot density map illustrates the estimated number of women aged 50–74 who were not counted as participants in BreastScreen Australia during 2023–2024.

Each dot represents approximately 1,000 women. Dots are randomly placed within their corresponding SA3 areas and do not show individual residential locations. Dot colours distinguish areas with higher and lower socioeconomic disadvantage, based on the ABS 2021 SEIFA Index of Relative Socio-economic Disadvantage (IRSD).

How to read this map: Denser clusters of dots indicate larger numbers of women not recorded as screening participants, not necessarily lower screening participation rates. Dot locations are simulated, and colours describe area-level socioeconomic disadvantage, not the circumstances of individual women.

Source: AIHW, BreastScreen Australia Monitoring Report 2026, Supplementary Table S1.12 (2023–2024); ABS, SEIFA 2021 (IRSD) and ASGS 2021 SA3 boundaries.

Does Where You Live Affect Your Likelihood of Being Screened?

Screening participation varies across different levels of geographic remoteness in Australia. Comparing major cities, regional areas, and remote communities reveals important differences in screening uptake.

In 2023–2024, women living in inner regional areas recorded the highest participation rate at 55.3%, while women in very remote areas recorded the lowest rate at 37.6%. This represents a difference of 17.7 percentage points.

Source: Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW), BreastScreen Australia Monitoring Report 2026, Appendix Table A1.4. Women aged 50–74, 2023–2024.

Are Women Returning for Regular Breast Cancer Screening?

Screening participation is only part of the story. Regular rescreening is also important for maintaining engagement with breast cancer screening programs. The chart below compares rescreening rates among women at different stages of their screening history.

Women who had already completed three or more screening rounds consistently recorded higher rescreening rates than women returning after their first screening. Rescreening rates declined during the periods affected by COVID-19, followed by a partial recovery.

Source: Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW), BreastScreen Australia Monitoring Report 2026, Appendix Table A2.1. Age-standardised rescreening rates among women aged 50–72, screening years 2014–2022. Rescreening is measured within 27 months. Note: Results for 2018–2022 were affected by COVID-19.

Source: Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW), BreastScreen Australia Monitoring Report 2026, Appendix Table A2.1. Age-standardised rescreening rates within 27 months among women aged 50–72, Australia, comparing 2017 and 2022.

Rescreening rates remained below their 2017 levels in 2022 across all three screening rounds. The second screening round experienced the largest decline, falling by 7.7 percentage points.

How Unequal Is Screening Participation Within Australian States?

State-level screening participation rates provide a useful overview, but they can conceal substantial differences between local communities.

This box plot examines the distribution of breast screening participation rates across Statistical Area Level 3 (SA3) regions within each Australian state and territory.

Each box represents the middle 50% of SA3 participation rates within a state or territory. The line inside the box shows the median, while individual points indicate potential outliers. The dashed reference line marks Australia's national participation rate of 52.4%.

Key finding: Screening participation varies not only between states and territories, but also between local communities within them. The Northern Territory shows a particularly low distribution of SA3 participation rates, while several other states contain regions with participation substantially below the national average.

Source: Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW), BreastScreen Australia Monitoring Report 2026. Women aged 50–74, 2023–2024. Each SA3 region contributes equally to the distribution. State-level box plot statistics are not population-weighted.

How Has Breast Cancer Incidence Changed Over Time?

Breast cancer incidence provides a broader perspective on the burden of disease beyond screening participation and detection. Among Australian women aged 50–74, the age-standardised incidence rate increased substantially between 1982 and 2021, although the trend varied over time.

The chart distinguishes observed incidence rates from estimates for 2022–2025. These estimates provide an indication of recent trends but should not be interpreted as confirmed cancer diagnoses.

Source: Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW), BreastScreen Australia Monitoring Report 2026, Appendix Table A7.6. Women aged 50–74, Australia, 1982–2025. Rates are age-standardised per 100,000 women. Data for 2022–2025 are estimates.

Where Is Breast Cancer Mortality Highest Across Australia?

Breast cancer mortality provides another perspective on the burden of disease across Australia. Between 2019 and 2023, age-standardised mortality rates among women aged 50–74 varied across states and territories.

Victoria recorded the highest mortality rate at 39.8 deaths per 100,000 women, while the Northern Territory recorded the lowest rate at 35.5. The proportional symbol map compares mortality rates across Australia's states and territories.

Geographic differences in mortality may reflect a range of factors and should not be interpreted as evidence that screening participation directly determines breast cancer mortality.

Source: Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW), BreastScreen Australia Monitoring Report 2026, Appendix Table A8.2. Women aged 50–74, 2019–2023. Age-standardised mortality rates per 100,000 women. Geographic boundaries: Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS), ASGS 2021.

Is Higher Screening Participation Associated with Lower Breast Cancer Mortality?

Screening participation and breast cancer mortality provide different perspectives on women's health across Australia. This scatter plot compares screening participation rates with age-standardised breast cancer mortality rates across Australia's eight states and territories.

The relationship is not straightforward. States and territories with higher screening participation do not consistently have lower mortality rates. The two measures also cover different reporting periods and may be influenced by population characteristics, cancer risk, diagnosis, treatment, and other factors.

Source: Australian Institute of Health and Welfare (AIHW), BreastScreen Australia Monitoring Report 2026. Screening participation: women aged 50–74, 2023–2024. Breast cancer mortality: women aged 50–74, 2019–2023. Mortality rates are age-standardised per 100,000 women. This comparison is descriptive and does not establish causation.
